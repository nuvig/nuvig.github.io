📐 TERPS, Demystified

Every instrument approach and departure you fly was drawn by hand-applying one 564-page rulebook: FAA Order 8260.3G, the United States Standard for Terminal Instrument Procedures. This page opens that rulebook up — the obstacle-clearance geometry behind every plate, why your minimums are the numbers they are, and what the same document tells controllers they may do with you.

What this document is Ch 1

Order 8260.3G — everyone calls it TERPS — is the standard the FAA (and the military services) must apply when designing any instrument procedure in US airspace: approaches, departures, STARs, airways, holding, and the controllers' vectoring charts. It's written for procedure designers, not pilots — which is exactly why it's worth reading as a pilot: every number on a plate is the output of a rule in here, and the rules are more knowable than they look. Effective 7/1/2024, 564 pages, cancels 8260.3F.

The one idea underneath everything: ROC

Required Obstacle Clearance is the contract. TERPS defines it as the minimum vertical protection the FAA considers satisfactory, valid only while the aircraft flies "normal operations" with systems working (2-1-2). The whole order is machinery for delivering ROC in two shapes:

  • Level surfaces — for segments flown at a fixed altitude. The ROC band sits on the highest obstacle in the segment: obstacle + ROC, rounded up, is your minimum altitude. The ladder (2-1-3): 1,000 ft en route / feeder / initial (2,000 in designated mountainous terrain) → 500 ft intermediate → 250–500 ft final. An approach is a funnel of shrinking guaranteed clearance.
  • Sloping surfaces — for climbs and glidepath descents. On a glidepath, the surface under you has slope 102 ÷ glidepath angle (a 3.00° glideslope is protected by a 34:1 surface). In a climb, ROC is 24% of your climb gradient — at the standard 200 ft/NM you keep just 48 ft/NM of clearance while the evaluation surface rises at 152 ft/NM (the famous 40:1 surface, 2-1-4).

Everything else — trapezoids, tables, formulas — is bookkeeping about where those surfaces apply and which obstacles count.

Primary and secondary areas

Almost every segment is a corridor with a primary area (full ROC) flanked by secondary areas where ROC tapers linearly from full value at the primary boundary to zero at the outer edge. The taper encodes a probability statement: you're very unlikely to be that far off course, so an obstacle out there needs less clearance. An obstacle just outside the secondary edge doesn't exist to the procedure at all. The Approach Anatomy tab lets you drag an obstacle through this and watch the ROC change.

The final approach course is designed first

Paragraph 2-1-8 is the design philosophy in one sentence: a procedure has at most four segments (initial, intermediate, final, missed) plus a circling area, and the final approach segment is designed first — "least flexible and most critical" — with everything else fitted around it. When a plate routes you somewhere strange, it's usually the scaffolding bending so the final doesn't have to.

What this order is not

8260.3G carries the conventional (ground-based navaid) criteria plus everything generic: minimums, departures, holding, MVAs. The RNAV/RNP approach criteria — LPV, LNAV/VNAV, RNP AR — live in the sibling Order 8260.58, which borrows this order's chapters 1–3 machinery. So a GPS approach's minimums and departure procedures still trace back here even though its final-segment geometry doesn't.

The lifecycle a pilot never sees

  • Anyone can trigger a procedure, but it flows through airspace analysis, airport-standards checks, obstacle surveys, and flight inspection of every navaid before publication (1-2-3).
  • A complete civil approach can never be issued by NOTAM (1-7-3) — full procedures only arrive on the 28-day publication cycle.
  • Deviations from criteria need a documented waiver showing an equivalent level of safety; the order insists "a nonstandard IFP is not substandard" (1-4-2). Military procedures that deviate without equivalent safety are marked "NOT FOR CIVIL USE" — on an NDB procedure that can literally mean 50 ft less obstacle clearance (6-2-4).
  • Weather reporting on the field is an alternate-airport requirement — destination minimums work from area weather plus a usable altimeter source (1-2-3.f).

Why plates are named the way they are (1-6)

  • Duplicate straight-in procedures to a runway take suffixes Z, Y, X… — assigned in reverse alphabetical order, so "Z" is just the first (usually primary) one, not a quality grade. "V" is reserved for simultaneous converging approaches.
  • Circling-only procedures use A through H, assigned forward — and the sequence is shared across all airports with the same city name in the same state. The three Atlanta airports share one sequence: a VOR-A at one field, NDB-B at the next, LDA-C at the third.
  • A "VOR-A" isn't a style choice: the final missed the straight-in alignment or descent-angle limits, and the letter tells you circling is all it's allowed to offer.

A localizer's protected area is measured in feet; a VOR's in miles — that, plus 50 ft of ROC, is the entire "why LOC minimums beat VOR minimums" story. The NDB's extra half-mile of width and 50–100 ft of ROC is the entire "why NDB minimums are high" story: an ADF bearing is simply trusted less, so more sky is protected and more obstacles get a vote. Adding a FAF to a VOR buys 50 ft of ROC (300→250) and a much slimmer trapezoid — why "VOR/DME" beats plain "VOR" to the same runway. Uniquely, a DME-arc final's straight-in MDA may never be lower than its own circling MDA (5-3-4.b(6)).

What makes it straight-in at all

Straight-in minimums require the final course within 30° of the runway (15° for arcs; 3° to keep the name "LOC" — one degree more and the identical antenna is charted as an LDA), crossing the extended centerline about 3,000 ft out, and a descent angle inside table 2-6-1's per-category caps (CAT A 5.70°, B 4.20°, C 3.77°, D 3.50°; floor 2.75°). Fail either test and the procedure survives as circling-only — that's the birth of every "VOR-A". Try it in the interactive: lower the FAF crossing distance until the VDA busts your category.

Course reversals — why the PT says what it says

  • "Remain within 10 NM" is the standard CAT B–D maneuvering allotment (2-4-5); 5 NM appears at CAT-A-only fields, 15 NM for CAT E or extra descent — and the PT distance grows 1 NM per 200 ft of extra altitude to lose.
  • PT completion altitude may sit at most 1,000–3,000 ft above the FAF crossing altitude (table 2-4-5, scaling with PT distance) — which is why PT altitudes always hug the profile.
  • A hold-in-lieu-of-PT over the FAF may be charted no more than 300 ft above the FAF altitude (2-4-5.e) — those holds feel "already on profile" because the rule forces them to be.

Fixes are areas, not points

Every intersection fix is a quadrangle of uncertainty, and protection starts at the earliest point you could believe you're there (2-9-9.b). System accuracies (2-9-6): VOR ±4.5° along the course you fly but ±3.6° for a crossing radial — the radial you're not flying is trusted more, because flight technical error only applies to the course being flown. DME: ±0.5 NM or 3%. A stepdown fix in the final segment earns publication only if it buys ≥60 ft of MDA or a visibility credit (2-9-9.c) — the level ROC band sits on the highest obstacle in the segment, and a stepdown is the designer's knife for cutting one rock out of the segment that holds the whole MDA hostage.

The missed approach, briefly

The 40:1 surface starts over the MAP at MDA minus the final ROC — the evaluation assumes you might arrive at the MAP having already eaten the entire final-segment clearance (2-8-5). Turning misseds are never designed below 400 ft above the field (2-8-6); if the numbers don't work, you get "climb to ___, then turn" — and that altitude is exactly what the 40:1 needs to out-climb the obstacle in the turn area, rounded up to 20 ft (2-8-8). On a standard 200 ft/NM climb you keep 48 ft/NM over the surface. ILS misseds are stricter still: the first 1,460 ft of the missed keeps the final's frozen surfaces and must not be penetrated at all (10-3).

Where the numbers on the minimums line come from Ch 3

Chapter 3 is the order's cash register: it converts the obstacle evaluation into the DA/MDA and visibility you brief. Almost nothing in it is arbitrary — most plate oddities are this chapter's arithmetic showing through.

The altitude side

  • MDAs round up in 20-ft steps; DAs in 1-ft steps (3-2-1). An MDA of 821 becomes 840. That alone explains most "odd" minimums.
  • HAT floors: 200 ft for CAT I ILS/LPV — but 250 ft for LNAV/VNAV, RNP AR, and LDA-with-glideslope, by rule, not obstacles (table 3-2-2). A 250-ft line next to a 200-ft ILS on the same runway isn't hiding a tower.
  • Steeper glidepath, higher floor: above 3.10° the minimum HAT climbs per category — at 3.6° a CAT D floor is 300 ft; past 3.80° CAT D is simply NA (table 3-2-2). And once a penetration forces a DA adjustment, the floor becomes (GPA/3)×250 — a steeper-than-3° ILS can never claw back a 200-ft DH.
  • Circling HAA floors: 350 ft CAT A, 450 B, 550 C/D (table 3-2-1); the circling MDA may never undercut the highest straight-in MDA on the same chart (3-2-1.h).
  • Remote altimeter: when the setting comes from more than 5 NM away, the penalty is a formula — 2.30 ft per NM + 0.14 ft per foot of terrain differential (3-2-2.c), and in adverse terrain the differential is measured across the whole 5-NM-wide corridor between source and field. That's how "when using X altimeter setting" notes add 80 ft at one airport and 700 at another. Hard caps: 75 NM, 6,000 ft differential. Alternate minimums never use remote-altimeter lines.
  • Long finals cost extra: an NPA final over 6 NM adds 5 ft of ROC per 0.1 NM (3-2-2.d). Precipitous terrain adds more (software-computed; +10% of HAT for precision approaches). Multiple adjustments sum before the single final rounding (3-2-2.e).

The visibility side — a contest, not a lookup

Published visibility is the highest of several independent tests (3-3-2): the table value for your HAT and lighting; the physical MAP-to-threshold distance; the visual-area surface evaluation; and runway-characteristic floors. Each has a story:

  • Lighting is credited by class, not hardware (table 3-1-2): FALS = ALSF-1/2, SSALR — and MALSR. The economy MALSR at a regional field buys the same ½-SM CAT I minimum as a hub's ALSF-2. IALS = MALS/SSALS/SALS family; BALS = ODALS; under 700 ft of lights = none.
  • The credit is literally geometric: with lights, the MAP-to-threshold distance test subtracts the light system's physical length — 2,400 ft for FALS, 1,400 IALS, 700 BALS (3-3-2.b). The lights extend the runway environment toward you by exactly their length.
  • Why ½ becomes ¾ when the ALS is out: at HAT 200–320, the FALS column reads ½ SM while every other column reads ¾ (table 3-3-1). The inop-components table is this difference.
  • RVR 1800 requires ILS/LPV/GLS plus touchdown-zone and centerline lights — and an offset final caps you at RVR 2400 no matter the equipment (table 3-3-1 notes).
  • Category changes the table: CAT A and B NPAs get their own friendlier tables (3-3-3/3-3-4 — ½ SM to HAT 880/740 with FALS); CAT C/D must pass a five-condition gate (table 3-3-5: not NDB, ≤5° offset, ≥3 NM final, a FAF, ≤8 NM FAF-to-threshold) to keep ½ — which is why one LOC line reads ½ for A/B and ¾ for C/D.
  • Runway characteristics set silent floors (table 3-3-6): no full-length parallel taxiway → 1 SM (waived while a tower is open — some plates literally say "visibility increased when tower closed"); no HIRL/MIRL → 1 SM; turf → ¾; runway shorter than 4,200 ft → ¾. A visibility minimum driven by taxiing.

The 34:1 and 20:1 visual surfaces — the night-restriction machinery

Beyond the electronic final there's a visual-segment check from the MDA/DA point to the runway (3-3-2.c). Penetrate the 34:1 surface and visibility floors at ¾ SM. Penetrate the 20:1 and: visibility floors at 1 SM, the VDP is deleted (a missing VDP on an NPA usually means exactly this), and if the obstacle is unlighted the chart denies the approach — or those minimums — at night. One unlighted 20:1 penetration on one straight-in kills night circling to that runway on every procedure at the airport (3-3-2.c(4)(b)). A VGSI can substitute for lighting the obstacle, which is why some fields' night authorization hangs on a PAPI staying in service.

Circling — a formula, not a table

Each category's circling area is built from arcs around each runway end with radius computed from true airspeed + a 25-kt wind pad at 20–25° of bank at the circling altitude (2-7-1) — at sea level roughly 1.3 / 1.8 / 2.8 / 3.7 NM for CAT A–D, growing with field elevation and MDA (the inverse-"C" icon on modern plates marks these expanded radii). ROC over the whole area: a flat 300 ft — the thinnest protection you'll ever maneuver under, which is the quantitative case for treating circling MDAs with respect. Circling visibility can never undercut the chart's straight-in visibility (3-3-3.d). "Circling NA east of Rwy 17-35" is a trade — a forbidden sector bought a lower MDA — and the excluded side still keeps a hidden 10° protective splay past each runway end (2-7-2).

Alternate and takeoff lines

  • Standard alternate minimums: 800-2 nonprecision, 600-2 precision — and LPV/LNAV-VNAV count as nonprecision here (3-4-1). The nonstandard-alternate triangle usually exists because of the line most pilots skip: the circling row, evaluated without light credit.
  • Standard takeoff minimums are real numbers: 1 SM (1–2 engines) / ½ SM (3+), from table 3-5-1. Everything else on the takeoff-minimums page is Chapter 13's obstacle story — see the Departures tab.
The departure bargain Ch 13, § 3-5

Every IFR departure rests on one assumption: you cross the departure end climbing at least 200 ft/NM, and TERPS draws a surface underneath you rising at 152 ft/NM — the 40:1. The gap is the "24% rule": ROC in a climb is 24% of the gradient, so at the standard climb you keep just 48 ft per NM over the evaluation surface. Everything on a takeoff-minimums page is what happens when an obstacle pokes through that 40:1.

The diverse departure — protection in every direction

First the runway gets an Initial Climb Area: 1,000 ft wide at the DER, splaying 15°, normally 2 NM long, evaluating the climb to 400 ft above the DER (13-1-2). Then the whole compass: Diverse A (everything ahead of a reference line 2,000 ft down the runway) takes the 40:1 from the ICA's end; Diverse B (everything behind it) takes a 40:1 that starts 400 ft above the airport — the design assumes you never turn below 400 AGL, which is where that habit's number comes from. Assessment runs to 25 NM (46 NM near designated mountainous terrain) (13-2-1). If nothing penetrates: no ODP is published — and the order forbids publishing one (13-2-2). Silence is the message: any heading, standard climb, protected.

When something penetrates — the designer's hierarchy (13-2-2)

  • Low, close-in obstacles (would need a gradient only to ≤200 ft above the DER): published as notes, not gradients — the order forbids fixing them with a climb gradient (3-5-3.a). The theory: you can see and avoid a 40-ft tree off the end. That's the whole "numerous trees beginning 200 ft from DER" section.
  • DER crossing height: penetrations ≤150 ft inside the ICA can be solved by requiring you to cross the DER at a stated height — "cross DER at or above 35 ft" is a measured penetration, to the foot (3-5-4).
  • Ceiling and visibility: a "300-1" style takeoff minimum is see-and-avoid over a specific charted obstacle — ceiling is never published at ≤200 ft, visibility never above 3 SM (3-5-2).
  • Nonstandard climb gradient: computed as CG = height ÷ (0.76 × distance) — the obstacle surface is drawn at only 76% of the published gradient, so an aircraft exactly making the number still banks 24% of it as clearance. The "to 2400" altitude is where the standard surface takes over. Gradients over 500 ft/NM need Flight Standards approval; beyond the hierarchy entirely: "an IFR departure must not be authorized."
  • Reduced takeoff runway length: penetrations ≤35 ft can be bought back by shortening the usable runway — about 30 ft of runway per foot of penetration (3-5-5).

ODP vs SID vs DVA — same geometry, different reasons to exist

  • An ODP exists only because obstacles penetrate the 40:1 — one may not be designed otherwise.
  • A SID exists because ATC asked for a route; its surfaces may be perfectly clean. Same Chapter 13 construction, opposite motivation.
  • A DVA lets ATC radar-vector departures below the MVA — but it is valid only during an uninterrupted climb from runway to MVA (13-5-1). Level off early and the DVA's guarantee is void by definition: its whole obstacle case is a sloping surface. DVAs also can't use ceiling/visibility as mitigation — climb gradient only (3-5-2.c).
  • "Climb to 800 before turning" pins the end of an extended ICA: turn protection is only constructed beyond it, so turning early exits every surface that was evaluated (13-1-4).

VCOA — the slow airplane's exit

Whenever a nonstandard gradient covers obstacles outside the ICA, the order requires a Visual Climb Over Airport option (13-4-1). Construction: take the highest obstacle in a visual climb circle around the airport (radius from a speed table — about 2 NM at 90–120 KIAS), run a 40:1 outward at least 19 NM, add 250 ft, round up — that's the published "climb to" altitude. The ceiling requirement is always that altitude + 100 ft (minimum 1,000); visibility is always 3 SM (5 SM if climbing to ≥10,000 MSL) — you can reverse-engineer a VCOA's climb-to altitude from its ceiling (3-5-2.b). It's the legal out when your airplane can't make the published gradient: spiral up visually over the field, then depart with normal protection.

Worked example — the whole machine in one obstacle

A 1,200-ft tower 3 NM from the DER (field at sea level). The 40:1 surface at 3 NM sits at 3 × 152 = 456 ft — penetrated by ~744 ft. Required gradient: 1200 ÷ (0.76 × 3) ≈ 527 ft/NM — above the 500 ceiling, so expect ceiling-and-visibility minimums or a VCOA instead of (or beside) the gradient. Move the same tower to 8 NM: surface at 1,216 ft, penetration 0 — no ODP at all. Distance is everything; the 40:1 forgives anything far enough away.

The parts of TERPS the controller flies for you Ch 11, 14–16

When you're on a vector, an airway, or in a hold, your obstacle protection still comes from this order — just from chapters whose output you never see charted. This is what the controller's copy guarantees, and where its edges are.

The MVA chart — the approach segments you can't see

  • The order explicitly lets a Minimum Vectoring Altitude chart replace the feeder, initial, and intermediate segments of a radar approach (11-3-2). A vector-to-final clearance carries no published altitudes because the controller's chart holds them.
  • Each sector guarantees 1,000 ft ROC (2,000 mountainous — reducible, never below 1,000) over every obstacle in the sector plus a 3–5 NM buffer past the sector boundary — 3 NM within 40 NM of a single sensor, 5 NM beyond or with multi-sensor data. Crossing a sector line early is protected by the buffer.
  • Isolation areas: any obstacle can be carved into its own 3–5-NM-radius high-MVA island so the surrounding sector can drop. An MVA that looks too low against a charted tower is usually an island you can't see.
  • Sector altitudes normally round to the next higher 100 ft, but nearest-100-ft rounding is allowed under tight conditions gated by a cold-temperature table: at an Average Cold Temperature of +7°C, 951 ft of clearance suffices; at −40°C the same rounding needs 1,851 ft (11-3-6). An obstacle-clearance rule with a climate input.
  • Why the MVA sits far below the MEA: the airway's MEA protects an 8-NM-wide corridor plus signal and communications margins; the MVA protects a radar-monitored sector with the same ROC and none of the nav-signal baggage.

Radar approaches — ASR and PAR

An ASR approach is a real procedure, not a courtesy: final ROC 250 ft — the same as a localizer — over an area whose width is the radar's lateral accuracy, with the controller issuing recommended altitudes each mile, in 20-ft increments, never below the MDA (11-2-5). PAR applies ILS criteria wholesale — same OCS, same DA logic, no fix tolerances (radar sees you continuously); military-only PARs may publish a 100-ft HAT (11-2-4).

The strangest fact in Chapter 15: breakout protection is borrowed from the opposite runway's departure assessment (15-4). "What happens if the monitor controller turns you off final" is evaluated as the reciprocal runway's diverse departure — and an allowed mitigation is simply drawing the obstacle on the controller's scope.

Holding — why the protected box is huge, and when it isn't yours

  • The AIM's maximum holding speeds (200/230/265 KIAS by altitude) are design inputs — patterns are sized for them, plus a 120-kt design wind and the assumption you achieve only 25° of bank even though you're told 30° ("a constant 30 degrees of bank is seldom achieved", 16-1-2).
  • Primary protected areas run from about 14 × 9 NM (slow, low) to 85 × 57 NM (FL500-class), plus a 2-NM secondary belt used only for obstacle math — ATC's charted holding shape shows the primary only (16-1-8).
  • Exceed the speeds, hold uncharted, or hold above the designed altitude and the box no longer applies — the order transfers obstacle responsibility to ATC in those cases, which in practice means: tell them.
  • A climb-in-hold pattern is evaluated at 310 KIAS with a 40:1 climb surface — why the chart annotation exists, and why you must climb continuously in one (16-7).
  • Turbulent-air protection (sized for 280 KIAS) exists only where charted — the airspace doesn't grow because it's bumpy (16-12).

En route, in four numbers

An airway is 4 NM of primary each side plus 2 NM of secondary, until VOR system accuracy (4.5°) overtakes the parallel boundary at 51 NM and the corridor starts widening (14-1-2). ROC is 1,000 ft — 2,000 mountainous, the order citing mountain-wave pressure deficits and the Bernoulli effect by name — reducible to 1,500/1,700 but never over man-made obstacles (14-2-1). MCAs assume you climb at only 150/120/100 ft/NM depending on altitude — gentler than the departure standard; the same airplane is assumed to climb worse the higher it is (14-3-1). And an MEA may legally contain a navigation-signal gap — up to ~65 NM at FL450 — that the chart never announces (14-4-1).

Ten things worth knowing that almost nobody knows
  • The 24% rule. On every climb — departure or missed — your obstacle clearance is 24% of your gradient. At the standard 200 ft/NM that's 48 ft per NM. One mile past the MAP you may be, legally, 48 ft above the evaluation surface.
  • 102 ÷ GPA. One constant ties every glidepath to its protecting surface. It's also why the fix for an obstacle under an ILS is to steepen the glideslope — the surface steepens with it.
  • MALSR is a "full" approach light system — the same visibility-credit class as ALSF-2 (table 3-1-2).
  • Crossing radials are trusted more than the radial you fly (±3.6° vs ±4.5°) — flight technical error only applies to the course being flown (2-9-6).
  • Circling protection carries a +25 kt wind pad and is recomputed for field elevation — the same CAT C circle is bigger in Denver than at sea level (2-7-1).
  • A DVA evaporates when your climb stops — vectors below the MVA are protected only during an uninterrupted climb (13-5-1).
  • Taxiing aircraft are obstacles. Below 800-2, ground traffic within 2 NM of an arrival formally penetrates the ILS final surfaces unless positive control keeps it clear — one reason CAT II operations hold short at the farther hold line (10-6-2).
  • PAPIs legally penetrate the OCS. Visual-aid infrastructure is exempted by name (table 10-6-1).
  • Holding assumes you can't hold 30° of bank — every pattern is sized for 25°, a 6-second fix-recognition delay, and a 120-kt design wind (16-1-2).
  • PRM breakout protection is the other runway's departure assessment — and "draw the obstacle on the controller's scope" is a legal mitigation (15-4).
Glossary
  • ROC — required obstacle clearance; the guaranteed vertical margin
  • OCS — obstacle clearance surface; the level or sloping sheet obstacles are measured against
  • OEA — obstacle evaluation area; where obstacles count at all
  • Primary / secondary area — full ROC / ROC tapering to zero at the edge
  • DA / MDA / CMDA — decision altitude (precision, 1-ft rounding) / minimum descent altitude (nonprecision, 20-ft rounding) / circling MDA
  • HAT / HAA — height above touchdown / above airport; what the tables index on
  • PFAF (FAF) — precise final approach fix; where final begins
  • MAP — missed approach point; where the 40:1 starts, at MDA − ROC
  • VDA — the computed nonprecision descent angle (min 2.75°, capped per category)
  • VDP — visual descent point; where the VGSI angle meets the MDA
  • GPA / TCH — glidepath angle / threshold crossing height
  • DER — departure end of runway; where the departure clock starts
  • ICA — initial climb area; 1,000 ft wide, 15° splay, to 400 ft above DER
  • ODP / SID / DVA — obstacle-driven departure / ATC-requested route / vector-below-MVA area
  • VCOA — visual climb over airport; the slow airplane's alternative to a gradient
  • MVA / MIA — minimum vectoring / IFR altitude; the controller's sector chart
  • RASS — remote altimeter setting source; 2.30 ft/NM + 0.14 ft/ft penalty
  • NTZ / PRM — no-transgression zone / precision runway monitor (close parallels)
  • FALS / IALS / BALS / NALS — full / intermediate / basic / no approach light system
  • 40:1 — the departure & missed climb surface: 152 ft/NM, i.e. 76% of the standard gradient
