🗺️ Approach Plate Decoder

An FAA instrument approach chart is a dense sheet where every mark means something, and almost none of it is labelled. This page rebuilds nine of them — three ordinary ones and six of the strangest in the national airspace system — and lets you click any item to find out what it is, why it is there, and what it is quietly telling you. Then compare them side by side and see what actually changes between a textbook ILS and a 6.5° descent into a Colorado valley.

⚠
Not for navigation. These are schematic teaching recreations, not copies of the published charts. The geometry — fixes, courses, altitudes, glidepath angles, runways, field elevations — is drawn live from the FAA CIFP cycle this site ships. Everything a CIFP file does not carry — frequencies, minimums, notes, TDZE, lighting — is authored by hand, marked ◊ illustrative in the explanation panel, and is not a substitute for the real plate. Every procedure links to its official chart. Terminal procedures change every 28 days.

    Every item on the plate

    Every column here is computed from the FAA CIFP cycle, not typed in: the final approach course is the bearing of the last final-segment leg converted to magnetic, the glidepath is the procedure's coded vertical angle, and the missed approach altitude is the climb constraint on the missed segment. Anything above 3.1° or a field above 5,000 ft is highlighted.

    How the drawings are built. Each plate names an airport and a procedure id; the page fetches data/procedures/apt/{ICAO}.json — the same FAA CIFP extract that powers the Procedure Explorer — decodes the ARINC 424 leg sequence, and renders the plan view, profile and briefing values from it. The final approach fix is identified as the last named fix before the leg where the coded vertical path begins; the missed approach point is the last fix of the final segment. Distances in the profile are straight-line between fixes, so a procedure built from curved RF legs reads slightly short.

    What is not real. Frequencies, minimums, procedure notes, TDZE and lighting are hand-authored to show what those boxes contain and where they sit — they are flagged in the explanation panel and must not be used to fly. Terrain contours and special-use airspace are drawn schematically. The chart reference and amendment numbers are placeholders.

    Reference material: FAA Instrument Procedures Handbook (FAA-H-8083-16), Instrument Flying Handbook (FAA-H-8083-15), the Aeronautical Chart User's Guide, and TERPS (FAA Order 8260.3). Nothing leaves your browser.
