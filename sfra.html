The DC SFRA

Washington's Special Flight Rules Area — the map, the rules, and a decision tree that walks any flight to its exact procedure · built from a CFI's seat at KANP, inside the ring · all tools →

1 the airspace→ 2 three rings→ 3 the requirements→ 4 from Lee→ 5 decision tree→ 6 when it breaks→ 7 how pilots bust it→ 8 the record

Since 2003, flying anywhere near Washington means flying inside national defense airspace — a 30 nm ring where every aircraft must be identified, talking, and squawking, wrapped around an inner zone where GA essentially may not go at all. The rules live in 14 CFR Part 93 Subpart V plus a Chart Supplement special notice, and violating them — even inadvertently — is handled as a security event, not a paperwork problem. This page draws the airspace to scale, translates the rules, and then lets you answer one question at a time until you're holding the exact procedure for your flight.

2 Three rings, three rulebooks

Work from the outside in: each ring keeps everything from the ring before and adds more.

60 nm — the training ring

DCA VOR/DME +60 nm
  • Flying VFR anywhere inside 60 nm? You must have completed the free FAA ALC-405 “DC SFRA” course and hold the completion certificate — one time, no expiration.
  • From 30–60 nm: ≤230 KIAS unless authorized.
  • No flight plan, code, or comms required out here — this ring exists so nobody near the SFRA can say they didn't know the rules.
91.161 · CS special notice. IFR flights are exempt from the training rule.

30 nm — the SFRA

Surface up to (not including) FL180
  • Before entering: file a DC SFRA flight plan, get a discrete code, establish two-way comms.
  • Squawk the code continuously; never 1200; Mode C required. Applies to everything — including ultralights.
  • ≤180 KIAS unless authorized.
  • Above FL180 (IFR by definition) the SFRA does not apply.
93.339 — the core operating rule.

~15 nm — the FRZ

Flight Restricted Zone · surface to FL180
  • GA flight is prohibited without FAA/TSA authorization — a filed plan and a code are not enough.
  • The only GA doorway: the vetted-pilot program for College Park (CGS) and Potomac Airfield (VKX) — fingerprints, background check, personal PIN. (Hyde Field, the third, closed in 2022.)
  • FRZ flight plans are filed by phone with Washington Center — no electronic filing.
93.341–93.343 · 49 CFR 1562.

P-56 — never

Monumental core & Naval Observatory · surface to 18,000 ft
  • P-56A: the Mall, White House, Capitol. P-56B: ½ mi around the Naval Observatory.
  • Prohibited, continuously, to everyone without Secret Service–level authorization. Inside the FRZ, which is inside the SFRA — three fences deep.
  • Nearby cousins: P-73 (Mount Vernon, sfc–1,500 ft) on the river route, and P-40/R-4009 (Camp David, 3 nm — expands by TFR when the President visits).
FAA Order JO 7400.10H.
3 What every VFR flight in the ring needs

§93.339 is a five-item checklist. Miss any one item and you're a track on a NORAD scope with fighters on alert — the rest of this page is just these five, applied.

1 · Training

ALC-405 certificate for any VFR flight within 60 nm. Free, online, ~1 hr, once ever. Show it to the FAA, TSA, or any law-enforcement officer on request.

91.161

2 · A DC SFRA flight plan

Its own type — not a regular VFR flight plan. File gate→airport, airport→gate, or gate→gate (Leidos / your EFB). It buys no search-and-rescue and no radar services; it exists so security knows who you are.

93.335, 93.339(a)(5)

3 · A discrete code — before entering

Getting the code is what activates the plan. You cannot enter first and sort it out airborne, and you cannot depart VFR planning to pick up an IFR clearance in the air. Squawk it continuously — 1200 is forbidden in the SFRA, even after “radar services terminated.”

93.339(a)(4), (e)

4 · Two-way comms, held throughout

Established with Potomac before the boundary and maintained the whole time. “Transponder observed” means the security system sees you — it is not flight following, and implies no traffic services at all.

93.339(a)(2) · CS notice phraseology

5 · Mode C + the speed limits

Altitude-reporting transponder required. ≤180 KIAS inside the ring, ≤230 KIAS from 30 to 60 nm — the limits that keep an unknown track's intercept math workable.

93.339(a)(3) · CS notice

The price of missing one

“Whether inadvertent or intentional”: civil penalties and certificate suspension or revocation; knowing violations of national defense airspace can be prosecuted criminally. There is no “I was only a mile inside.”

93.333 · 49 U.S.C. 46307
4 From Lee Airport, in practice

KANP sits about 17 nm east of the DCA VOR — inside the SFRA, outside the FRZ, non-towered. Every takeoff and landing at Lee is an SFRA operation. The numbers that run a Lee flight:

The shape of every VFR departure

  1. File the DC SFRA plan: ANP → your exit gate.
  2. Phone Potomac Clearance from the ramp → discrete code + departure frequency.
  3. Squawk the code before takeoff. Announce on CTAF, depart.
  4. Call Potomac climbing out; stay with them to the boundary.
  5. Cross the 30 nm ring, get well clear, then turn on course. The plan closes itself at the boundary.
Arrivals are the mirror image — code and comms before the ring, plan closes on landing. The decision tree below has every variant with the details.

    6 When something breaks

    The SFRA has published answers for every failure — the theme is always the same: stay predictable, talk if you can, and get out by the shortest path if you can't.

    Radios fail

    Squawk 7600 and exit the SFRA by the most direct lateral route — or return to your departure point if that's closer (in the FRZ, only if within 5 nm of it).

    CS special notice

    Transponder fails

    Tell ATC immediately and do what they say. If you can't reach them, same drill: most direct lateral route out, or back to the departure point if closer.

    CS special notice

    You're intercepted

    Follow the interceptor's instructions — all of them, immediately. Come up on 121.5 with call sign and position, squawk 7700 unless directed otherwise. Rock your wings back if signaled. This is not the moment to explain yourself first.

    AIM 5-6-2 · CS notice

    The lasers — Visual Warning System

    Red–red–green flashes aimed at your aircraft mean security suspects you're in the SFRA/FRZ without authorization. Eye-safe, visible 15–20 mi.

    Talking to ATC? Tell them immediately that you're being illuminated. Not talking to anyone? Turn away from DC now and call ATC or 121.5.

    AIM 5-6-16 · fielded by NORAD, 2005

    A real emergency

    Declaring an emergency (§91.3(b)) or flying IFR lost-comms procedures (§91.185) is a defense to the training rule and takes priority — aviate first. Expect to talk about it afterward; expect an escort if it takes you toward the FRZ.

    91.161(d)
    7 How pilots actually bust it

    GAO found pilot error — not bad actors — behind the overwhelming majority of restricted-airspace violations here. The recurring mistakes:

    • Squawking 1200 in the pattern. Pattern work at a non-towered SFRA field (Lee!) needs a filed pattern plan and a discrete code from Potomac plus CTAF calls. The famous “1234” is the towered-airport pattern code only.
    • Cutting the corner. The ring is a circle and your course line probably isn't. Turning on course right at the boundary — or clipping the arc while going around DC — is the classic deviation. Exit, get well clear, then turn.
    • Trying to fix it airborne. The discrete code must exist before you cross the boundary — there is no “pick up a code in the air,” VFR or IFR.
    • Reverting to 1200 when “radar services” end. The code stays until you land or exit. Muscle memory is the enemy on this one.
    • Believing flight following covers it. An SFRA plan gives no SAR and no radar services; flight following gives no SFRA compliance. They are unrelated systems.
    • The 60 nm surprise. The training rule catches VFR pilots who never touch the SFRA — transiting 55 nm out without ALC-405 is itself a violation.
    • Fringe-airport 1205 inbound. The 1205 shortcut is outbound only. Arriving at Barnes, Robinson, or Skyview means the full SFRA procedure.
    • Trusting old references. The CFR still lists five fringe airports and three FRZ airports; the chart notice says three and two (Hyde closed 2022). Frequencies move. The current TAC, Chart Supplement, and NOTAMs outrank everything — including this page.
    • P-40 on a Camp David weekend. The little 3 nm circle near Thurmont grows to ~10 nm by TFR when the President is there — a northbound trap on the way to Gettysburg. Brief it every time.
    How it got this way — ADIZ to SFRA

    Feb 2003: with Iraq looming, the DC ADIZ appears overnight by NOTAM — “temporary,” enormous, and instantly the most-violated airspace in the country. May 2005: a lost Cessna 150 gets within 3 mi of the White House; the Capitol evacuates; the pilots meet a Black Hawk, an F-16's flares, and the national news.

    Dec 2008: after >20,000 overwhelmingly opposed public comments, the FAA makes it permanent anyway — the ADIZ becomes the SFRA of 14 CFR Part 93 Subpart V, effective Feb 17, 2009, shrunk to the 30 nm ring. The training rule (91.161) lands the same month: proposed at 100 nm, finalized at 60. The Visual Warning System lasers had been fielded in 2005. Nothing about it has been temporary since.

    The gates & sector frequencies (FAA course table — verify on the current TAC)

    A gate is nothing exotic: it's an ordinary NAS intersection (or navaid — BRV is the Brooke VORTAC) pressed into service as a filing fix. What the gate really names is a slice of the 30 nm boundary, bounded by DCA VOR radials — WOOLY is R-341 clockwise to R-046, PALEO picks up at R-047, and so on around the ring. You file to/from the gate and cross the boundary anywhere in its slice; you never have to fly to the fix itself (several sit well outside the ring — the map draws each fix, its slice, and the dotted tie between them).     8 The violation record

    Two very different datasets. The official counts are fragmentary — they surface in GAO testimony and congressional hearings, then go dark (FAA's per-year deviation and enforcement numbers live in internal systems reachable only by FOIA). The continuous record is NASA ASRS: the voluntary, de-identified reports pilots file about their own incursions and close calls — a shadow of the true count, but a shadow with narratives.

    Read this data honestly — what ASRS is and isn't

    ASRS is voluntary and immunity-driven. Pilots file partly because a timely report waives most enforcement sanctions — so filing behavior tracks fear and awareness, not the violation rate. Compare the FAA's 998 counted violations in 2003 with the 413 ASRS reports here that even mention the airspace.

    A mention is not a violation. The set is every report whose narrative or synopsis contains SFRA, ADIZ, FRZ, or P-56 — that includes near-misses, controllers' reports, and procedural gripes; the “violation-flagged” filter narrows to reports ASRS itself coded as an airspace violation. Hits before 2003 are mostly the coastal ADIZ — the term predates Washington's.

    De-identification is by design. Dates are year-month only, no tail numbers, no names. Each row's ACN is the report's accession number in the ASRS Database Online, where the full narrative is public. Data is a point-in-time export; the archive updates when the export is re-run.
