Quick take

From balloon to forecast — how the two views connect

Twice a day, at 00Z and 12Z, about 92 U.S. National Weather Service offices release weather balloons. Each radiosonde climbs at roughly 1,000 ft/min for about two hours, radioing back pressure, temperature, and humidity while GPS drift gives the winds — one true measured column of atmosphere, up to ~100,000 ft. The SPC chart above is that raw ascent drawn by their SHARP analysis package, with the severe-weather math laid out around it. Our nearest launch site is Sterling VA (IAD), about 40 nm west of Annapolis.

Assimilation: the balloon teaches the model

Those observations don't stay on a chart. Every RAOB — along with satellite radiances, aircraft reports, surface stations, and radar — is fed into data assimilation, the step where a model's previous forecast is nudged toward what was actually measured. Radiosondes are disproportionately valuable here: they are one of the few direct, full-column truth measurements, so they anchor the satellite data that fills the gaps between launch sites. The corrected 3-D snapshot (the analysis) becomes the starting point from which the GFS, HRRR, ICON and friends integrate the equations of motion forward in time.

From grid to the diagram at the top of this page

The model's output is a grid — for the GFS, roughly 13 nm between points; for the HRRR, about 1.6 nm — with values stored at standard pressure levels (1000, 975, 950 … 100 hPa). When you type an airport code above, the page looks up its coordinates, and Open-Meteo pulls the model column nearest that point: temperature, humidity, wind, and geopotential height at each level. This page then reconstructs dewpoint from humidity, lifts a surface parcel, and draws the same kind of diagram SPC draws — except the "balloon" is virtual, it exists everywhere, and it exists for tomorrow too.

Why they disagree — and which to trust

  • Resolution — the balloon reports every few seconds of ascent and captures razor-thin inversions and moist layers; the model column is smoothed onto ~21 levels here. A 300-ft-thick freezing-drizzle layer can be invisible in the model sounding.
  • Time and place — the RAOB is truth, but only at 00Z/12Z and only at the launch site (and the balloon drifts downwind as it climbs). The model column is an estimate, but for any hour and any point.
  • Drift with lead time — a model sounding valid an hour from now is usually excellent; at 48 h, timing errors in fronts and moisture grow. Comparing the 12Z IAD balloon against the model's 12Z column (set the slider above to the same hour) is the honest way to judge how much to trust today's forecast soundings.

Good habit: check the morning IAD balloon here, note where the inversions and moisture really are, then watch how the forecast soundings evolve them through the day.

How to read a Skew-T

A Skew-T log-p diagram plots one column of atmosphere: pressure decreases upward on a log scale (so the vertical axis is roughly linear in altitude), and isotherms are skewed 45° to the right — which is why the temperature trace of a normal atmosphere runs nearly straight up the page instead of leaning hard left.

The two data traces

Temperature and dewpoint, plotted against height. The horizontal gap between them is the moisture story: where they nearly touch, the air is saturated — that's a cloud layer or fog. Where they spread wide, the air is dry. Wind barbs on the right edge show speed/direction at each level (each full barb = 10 kt, flag = 50 kt).

The background reference lines

  • Isotherms — constant temperature, skewed 45°. The 0 °C line matters most: it's the freezing level.
  • Dry adiabats — the path an unsaturated rising parcel cools along (~3 °C / 1,000 ft).
  • Moist adiabats — the slower cooling of a saturated parcel (condensation releases heat).
  • Mixing-ratio lines — constant water-vapor content, in g/kg.

The parcel and CAPE

The dashed white line lifts a surface parcel: dry-adiabatically to the LCL (where it saturates — a good estimate of cumulus cloud base), then moist-adiabatically above. Where the parcel is warmer than the environment it accelerates upward — that area is shaded red and totals to CAPE (convective fuel). Where it's colder, shaded blue, is CIN — the cap that must be broken before storms can fire.

What a pilot looks for

  • Cloud bases — LCL for cumulus; any level where T and Td pinch together marks a layer of cloud or high humidity.
  • Stability — big CAPE with weak CIN on a summer afternoon means building cumulus and possible thunderstorms; a strong inversion (temperature increasing with height) traps haze and turbulence below it and gives smooth air above.
  • Icing — visible moisture between 0 °C and about −20 °C is the structural-icing band; check where the traces are close together in that temperature range.
  • Freezing level — where the temperature trace crosses 0 °C; drives icing and precipitation-type decisions.
  • Wind shear — barbs changing speed or direction sharply over a short height, especially near the surface at dawn (low-level jet above a nocturnal inversion), warn of LLWS on climbout and mechanical turbulence.
