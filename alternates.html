🛬 Alternate Rules & Advisement

Every FAA rule about alternate airports — who must file one, when the requirement goes away, what weather a candidate must be forecast to have, and how the answer changes between Part 91, 121, 135 and 125. Each part gets its own tab with the rules laid out and an interactive flowchart: answer the questions and it walks your exact situation to a verdict.

General aviation IFR. Two regulations carry the whole subject: §91.167 (the fuel you must carry) and §91.169 (when the flight plan must name an alternate, and what weather that alternate must be forecast to have) — plus the AIM's GPS/WAAS planning rules, which decide whether the approach you're counting on at the alternate may be satellite-based at all. The flowchart walks all three: requirement, equipment, weather.

The fuel rule §91.167

No IFR operation unless you carry enough fuel (considering weather reports, forecasts and conditions) to:

  1. Fly to the first airport of intended landing;
  2. Fly from there to the alternate — when one is required; and
  3. Fly after that for 45 minutes at normal cruising speed (30 minutes for helicopters).

The alternate leg drops out of the fuel calculation only when the §91.169(b) exception — the flowchart's "1-2-3" branch — applies.

The 1-2-3 exception §91.169(b)

An alternate must be filed unless both are true:

  • The destination has at least one published instrument approach (a Part 97 procedure, or a special IAP issued to you); and
  • For 1 hour before through 1 hour after your ETA, forecasts show ceiling at least 2,000 ft above the field and visibility at least 3 SM.

Helicopters: from ETA through ETA + 1 hour, ceiling at least 1,000 ft above the field — or 400 ft above the lowest applicable approach minima, whichever is higher — and visibility at least 2 SM.

Qualifying the alternate §91.169(c)

The candidate must be forecast, at your ETA at the alternate, to meet:

  • Non-standard minima published? The TPP's Alternate Minimums section (▲A on the plate) overrides the standard numbers — and "NA" means the airport can't be filed as an alternate at all (arrival that way, at least).
  • Otherwise, standard: precision approach 600-2 · nonprecision 800-2. (LPV counts as nonprecision for this planning — see the WAAS card.)
  • No instrument approach at the candidate? Still legal if the ceiling and visibility let you descend from the MEA, approach, and land under basic VFR.

Helicopters: 200 ft above the minima for the approach to be flown, visibility at least 1 SM but never below the procedure's own minimum.

WAAS & GPS at the alternate AIM 1-1-17 / 1-1-18

  • Non-WAAS GPS (TSO-C129/C196): with FDE and a preflight RAIM prediction, you may base planning on a GPS approach at the destination or the alternate — not both. The other end needs a conventional approach you're equipped to fly, ground station operational. At the alternate you may plan against the LNAV or circling MDA (LNAV/VNAV with approved baro-VNAV; RNP 0.3 if authorized).
  • WAAS (TSO-C145/146): RNAV (GPS) may be planned at both ends — but the alternate's weather test uses the LNAV or circling line (LNAV/VNAV with approved baro-VNAV), or a conventional procedure with "or GPS" in the title. Never the LPV line: for planning it's nonprecision 800-2; on arrival you fly the LPV.
  • Watch the ▲A listing and NOTAMs for GPS-specific "NA" notes, and think twice when both the destination and the alternate are GPS-only airports — one constellation problem takes out plan A and plan B together.

What the alternate is — and isn't

The alternate is a planning and fuel requirement, not a commitment. It lives in the flight plan; ATC will not automatically clear you there, and once airborne you may divert to any airport that makes sense. The regulation's job is to guarantee that at your planning moment there existed somewhere you could realistically get into, with fuel to reach it.

Local note: KANP has no instrument approach, so an IFR arrival to KANP always requires an alternate — no forecast is good enough to waive it.

Airline flying. Part 121 splits the alternate question three ways by kind of operation — domestic (§121.619), flag (international, §121.621) and supplemental (charter/cargo, §121.623) — adds a takeoff alternate (§121.617), and replaces the 600-2/800-2 alternate test with derived minima from the operator's OpSpecs (§121.625). Dispatch shares responsibility: every required alternate must be listed in the dispatch or flight release.

Takeoff alternate §121.617

If departure weather is below the landing minimums in the operator's OpSpecs for that airport — you could leave but not come back — the release must name a takeoff alternate:

  • 2 engines: within 1 hour at normal cruise, still air, one engine inoperative;
  • 3+ engines: within 2 hours, same conditions.

Its weather must meet the OpSpec alternate minima when you'd arrive.

Destination alternate, by operation

Domestic §121.619: at least one alternate unless the 1-2-3 test passes (same numbers as Part 91). When destination and first alternate are both forecast marginal, a second alternate must be designated.

Flag §121.621: alternate required unless the flight is scheduled ≤ 6 hours and, ETA ±1 h: ceiling ≥ 1,500 ft above the lowest circling MDA (no circling: 1,500 above the lowest published minimum or 2,000 above the field, whichever greater) and visibility ≥ 3 mi (or lowest landing vis + 2, whichever greater). Island routes approved with no alternate: carry fuel to destination + 2 hours at normal cruise.

Supplemental §121.623: an alternate is always required under IFR or over-the-top — no weather escape — except approved no-alternate routes outside the lower 48, with fuel per §§121.643/121.645.

Derived alternate minima §121.625 · OpSpec C055

The carrier's alternate weather test comes from its OpSpecs, and the standard C055 formula replaces 600-2/800-2:

  • One usable approach (one suitable runway): add 400 ft to the DA/MDA and 1 SM to the visibility of the approach to be used;
  • Two usable approaches to different suitable runways: add 200 ft and ½ SM to the higher of the two DA/MDAs and visibilities.

Same formula pattern applies to 135 and 125 operators — it rewards airports with redundant approaches, which is exactly what you want in an alternate.

Fuel & the conditional-language problem

Fuel: domestic — destination, then most-distant required alternate, then 45 min (§121.639). Turbine flag — destination + 10 % of total time, alternate, then 30 min hold at 1,500 ft (§121.645).

Exemption 3585: when a TAF's TEMPO/PROB language dips below minima while the main body is at or above them, carriers holding this long-standing exemption may still dispatch — with a second alternate and tighter fuel. Without it, conditional language below minima kills the release. (Part 91 flying has no such relief — see Advisement.)

Charter and air taxi. Part 135 gates the departure on the destination's own forecast (§135.219 — you can't even launch IFR toward a below-minimums destination), requires a takeoff alternate at 1 hour normal cruise (§135.217), uses a 1,500-above-minimums test instead of Part 91's 1-2-3 (§135.223), and sends the alternate's weather test to the OpSpecs (§135.221).

Before you can even go §135.219 · §135.217

§135.219: no IFR takeoff (and no beginning an IFR or over-the-top segment) unless reports/forecasts show the destination at or above authorized IFR landing minimums at your ETA. Part 91 has no equivalent — a 91 pilot may legally launch at a below-minimums destination and "take a look."

§135.217: departure weather at/above takeoff minimums but below authorized IFR landing minimums → a takeoff alternate within 1 hour at normal cruising speed, still air. Note: no one-engine-inoperative condition here, unlike 121/125.

Destination alternate §135.223

Fuel must cover destination → alternate → 45 min at normal cruise (helicopters 30 min). The alternate — and its fuel leg — drop out only if:

  • The destination has a Part 97 standard instrument approach (a special won't do), and
  • ETA ±1 h: ceiling ≥ 1,500 ft above the lowest circling MDA — or, no circling authorized, 1,500 ft above the lowest published minimum or 2,000 ft above the field, whichever higher — and visibility ≥ 3 mi, or 2 mi above the lowest applicable minimum, whichever greater.

Qualifying the alternate §135.221

Airplanes: forecast at/above the authorized alternate minima in the OpSpecs at your ETA there — the derived C055-style numbers described on the 121 tab, not 600-2/800-2.

Rotorcraft: at ETA, ceiling 200 ft above the minima for the approach to be flown and visibility at least 1 SM (never below the procedure's minimum); with no IAP at the alternate, ceiling/vis must allow descent from the MEA, approach and landing under basic VFR — the same structure Part 91 gives helicopters.

The quiet one: Part 125 covers large airplanes (20+ passenger seats or 7,500 lb+ payload) operated noncommercially — corporate shuttles, private 737s. Its alternate rules read like Part 121 supplemental: no weather exception at all for the destination alternate.

Departure alternate §125.365

Departure weather below the OpSpec landing minimums → the flight release must name an alternate within 1 hour (2 engines) or 2 hours (3+ engines) at normal cruise, still air, one engine inoperative — the same shape as §121.617.

Destination alternate §125.367 · §125.369

Every release under IFR or over-the-top lists at least one alternate per destination. There is no 1-2-3 and no 6-hour escape — the only out is an approved no-alternate route outside the lower 48, with fuel per §§125.375/125.377. The alternate's forecast must meet the OpSpec alternate weather minima at arrival (§125.369), and every required alternate is listed in the flight release.

The same four questions, across every part. The pattern to notice: as operations get more commercial, the escape hatches narrow (1-2-3 → 1,500-above-minimums → none) while the alternate weather test moves from fixed numbers to OpSpec-derived ones.

The rules above are the floor. This tab is the judgment layer — how to read forecasts against them, the GPS fine print, and how to pick an alternate you'd actually be glad to see.

Read the whole TAF — TEMPO counts

FAA legal interpretations treat conditional forecast language as controlling: if a TEMPO or PROB group drops the destination below 2,000-3 in your window, the 1-2-3 exception fails and you file the alternate; if it drops your candidate below its alternate minima at ETA, the candidate doesn't qualify. Part 121 carriers buy relief through Exemption 3585; Part 91 and most 135 operators have none — plan to the worst line of the forecast.

▲A, NA, and the NOTAM check

Before filing any alternate, open the TPP Alternate Minimums section (▲A) and the NOTAMs. Common traps:

  • "NA" — alternate use not authorized, usually an unmonitored navaid or no local weather reporting;
  • Conditions like "NA when tower closed" or "NA when local weather not available" — an airport that qualifies at 2 PM may be unusable at 2 AM;
  • A navaid outage NOTAM can turn the only non-GPS approach off — which matters for the GPS rules in the next card.

GPS & WAAS at the alternate AIM 1-1-17 / 1-1-18

  • Non-WAAS GPS (TSO-C129/C196): with FDE and a RAIM check, you may plan a GPS-based approach at the destination or the alternate — not both. The other airport needs an approach you can fly conventionally, with the ground equipment operational.
  • WAAS (TSO-C145/146): you may plan RNAV (GPS) approaches at both — but the alternate planning must use the LNAV or circling line (LNAV/VNAV with approved baro-VNAV), never the LPV line. So a WAAS airplane plans the alternate as nonprecision: 800-2, then happily flies the LPV on arrival.
  • Plates whose alternate minima carry the "N/A" against GPS notes, and airports with GPS-only approaches, deserve extra attention when the destination is also GPS-only.

Legal ≠ smart

  • 600-2 at the alternate means an approach to minimums after a diversion — tired, low on fuel, weather trending who-knows. Personal minima belong here more than anywhere.
  • Pick an alternate under different weather — upwind of the front, out of the fog bank — not just the nearest legal field. If one air mass took out the destination, it took out everything nearby.
  • Think services: fuel at 11 PM, an FBO, ground transport, an ILS rather than the bare legal minimum approach.
  • 45 minutes of reserve is a floor, not a plan. Diversions come with vectors, weather deviations and go-arounds; carry real margin.

Filed vs flown

The filed alternate binds your planning, not your flying. ATC doesn't hold it in your clearance, and when the miss happens you divert wherever is best then — which may well not be the filed field. Brief the realistic divert before the approach: with two-crew operations that's the "what are we doing at minimums" call; single-pilot, it's the same call, made on the ground when you're smart.

Nothing forbids filing an alternate when none is required — on a marginal 2,500-and-4 day it costs one line of the flight plan and buys a pre-thought-out plan B.

The KANP angle

  • KANP has no instrument approach → an IFR flight plan to KANP always names an alternate, in any weather. Common picks with real approaches and TAF coverage: KBWI, KMTN, KDCA — or KESN/KSBY across the Bay under a different air mass.
  • KANP can serve as someone's alternate only through the no-IAP rule: weather allowing descent from the MEA and a basic-VFR landing (§91.169(c)(2)).
  • KANP has no on-field weather and no TAF — plan off the KMTN/KBWI/KDCA TAFs and KNAK obs, the same stations this site's weather pages watch.
