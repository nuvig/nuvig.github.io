How the machine works

Everything on this page is one particle system: each dot is a nominal one gram of water, and it obeys a simplified but honest version of atmospheric thermodynamics. Nothing is scripted — clouds flatten at the condensation level, rain falls on the windward slope, and fog forms on cold nights because the physics says so.

Latent heat — the cycle's currency

Evaporating one gram of water costs about 2,260 joules. That energy doesn't vanish — it hides in the vapor as latent heat and travels with it. When the vapor later condenses into a cloud droplet, the same 2,260 J is released, warming the surrounding air. This is why the sea surface cools as it evaporates (watch the "latent heat uptake" bar), and why growing clouds get an extra buoyant kick the moment condensation begins (the "latent heat release" bar). A thunderstorm is a machine for moving ocean heat into the upper atmosphere.

evaporation: water + 2,260 J → vapor  ·  condensation: vapor → water + 2,260 J

Why cloud bases are flat

A rising parcel of dry air cools at almost exactly 9.8 °C per kilometre (the dry adiabatic lapse rate) — not by mixing with cold air, but because it expands into lower pressure. Its dew point falls much more slowly (~2 °C/km). The two meet at a predictable altitude, the lifted condensation level:

LCL ≈ 125 m × (T − Tdew)

Every parcel that starts near the surface saturates at nearly the same height — so the bottoms of the clouds line up along it. Drag the humidity slider: moist air (small T−Td) puts the cloud base low; dry air pushes it high. The dashed line in the scene marks the current LCL.

Lapse rates and instability

The environment in this model cools with height at 6.5 °C/km. A dry parcel cooling at 9.8 loses its warmth advantage quickly — it rises only as far as its initial surface heating carries it. But once condensation begins, latent heat release slows the parcel's cooling to about 6 °C/km (the moist adiabatic rate) — slightly less than the environment. The parcel stays warmer than its surroundings and accelerates: this is conditional instability, and it's why afternoon cumulus over hot land can suddenly bloom into towers while morning parcels fizzle below the LCL.

The pressure picture

Press the Pressure button. Land heats and cools fast; the sea barely moves. On a sunny afternoon the warm column over land expands, so pressure surfaces aloft bulge upward — and surface pressure under the warm column falls a few hectopascals (a thermal low). Air near the surface then flows from the cooler high (sea) toward the warmer low (land): the sea breeze, with a weak return flow aloft. At night the land cools below the sea temperature and the circulation reverses into a land breeze. The isobar bulges drawn on screen are vertically exaggerated so you can see them.

Mountains: forced lift and the rain shadow

Wind hitting the mountain has nowhere to go but up. That forced (orographic) lift pushes moist air through its LCL whether it's buoyant or not — so with any onshore wind the windward slope wears a cap of cloud and takes the rain. Descending the far side, the now-dried air warms at the full dry rate, arriving warmer and drier than it left: the rain shadow, and the reason foehn/chinook winds are warm. Set wind to ~20 kt and watch the two sides of the mountain live in different climates.

Night: fog and the reverse cycle

After sunset the land radiates its heat away. If it cools to the dew point, vapor condenses right at the surface — radiation fog, which is just a cloud whose LCL reached the ground. High humidity and calm, clear nights make it easiest. At sunrise the fog "burns off": the sun re-supplies the latent heat that condensation released.

What the model simplifies

This is a teaching model: 2-D, a fixed 6.5 °C/km environment, no Coriolis, cartoon microphysics (droplets become rain by crowding, not true collision–coalescence), and pressure drawn from column temperature alone. The numbers quoted — 2,260 J/g, 9.8 and ~6 °C/km, LCL ≈ 125 m×(T−Td) — are the real ones.
